## Coefficient of Friction: Tire-on-Wet/Submerged Pavement
Two categories of relevant primary/peer-reviewed data were found:

1. **Persson-type rubber friction theory (Persson & co-authors, arXiv/peer-reviewed physics literature)**: wet rubber friction on rough substrates is typically **20–30% lower** than dry friction at low sliding velocities, attributed to a "sealing effect" where rubber traps water pools that smooth the effective substrate roughness, reducing viscoelastic (hysteresis) friction contribution. This is a mechanistic/theoretical framework rather than a single point-value CoF, so it must be applied as a multiplier against your dry-pavement baseline rather than as a standalone μ.[^7][^8]
2. **Surface Free Energy (SFE) tribology study** on rubber-basalt (proxy for asphalt) interfaces under wet conditions using a British Pendulum Tester found wet adhesive friction is the dominant CoF contributor, with dry adhesion contributing minimally, and validated a mechanistic-empirical model against measured wet CoF (R² ≈ 0.95), though the paper does not report a single generalized wet μ range applicable across all rubber/pavement combinations.[^12]
3. **Rolling resistance coefficients** (distinct from sliding/skid friction): dry rubber-asphalt 0.010–0.018 (avg 0.014), wet 0.0022–0.005 (avg 0.0036). This metric is not the same as the tire-ground sliding/traction coefficient needed for a rigid-body contact model and should not be substituted for it.[^9]

No single, authoritative, primary-source numeric range specifically for "rubber tire on **submerged** asphalt/concrete" (as opposed to merely wet-surface, non-submerged) was located in this research. Standard pavement skid-resistance testing (ASTM E274 locked-wheel or E501/E524 methods) was referenced indirectly but full ASTM figures were not retrieved as primary sources; this is an **explicit gap** — for a submerged/flooded roadway scenario specifically, dedicated hydroplaning/water-film-thickness literature (e.g., NASA hydroplaning equations referenced in tire-pavement FE hydroplaning studies) is the more appropriate primary source category, and it was only partially retrieved.[^13]
