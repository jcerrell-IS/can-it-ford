## 1. CLOSED. Does Smith, Modra and Felder (2019) contain an Equation 6?

**No. The paper's equations run to (4), and Equation (4) solves for flow velocity V.**

Route that worked: Scite full-text excerpts by DOI. Scite reports the paper as **open access,
bronze**, with `access.url = https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1111/jfr3.12527`.
The prior `UNVERIFIABLE-Wiley-blocked` record was a session-specific Cloudflare challenge, not
a property of the source. No bot challenge was bypassed; Scite's own full-text index was used
instead. The paper is **not** in `~/Zotero/storage/`, so no local route existed.

Verbatim excerpts:

> "Combining Equations (1)–(3), and rearranging in terms of the flow velocity V resulted in an
> equation for which the vehicle remained stable: where f μ is a dimensionless factor allowing
> the adjustment of the traction forces measured on smooth wet concrete to the worst-case
> friction coefficient μ = 0.3 determined by the direct measurement of the friction
> coefficients."

> "Using the measured traction force for the various flow depths as well as the measured
> friction and drag coefficients, Equation (4) provided the flow velocity V."

> "(The adjustment of μ was conducted through the dimensionless factor f μ in Equation (4).)"

**Answers to the four sub-questions:**

1. An "Equation 6" is not evidenced. The terminal stability equation is **(4)**.
2. Equation (4) yields a **flow velocity V in m/s**, not a displacement.
3. The paper defines instability by **stability curves in depth-velocity space**, built from
   directly measured traction, friction and drag forces. It does **not** define instability by
   an absolute lateral displacement or drift distance at any value.
4. Scite tally: **total 51, supporting 1, contrasting 0, mentioning 49, across 42 citing
   publications. No retraction, correction, or expression of concern.**

Two additional measured quantities recovered, both usable: **average drag coefficient
C_D = 1.38**, and friction coefficients **μ = 0.78 on wet concrete** against a **worst-case
μ = 0.3**.

The paper's removal of the "Eq. 6" attribution was correct. It is now grounded positively
rather than merely by absence.

## 2. CLOSED. Does any peer-reviewed source define instability by a ~0.05 m drift distance?

**No.** Re-confirmed independently this pass with my own Consensus query rather than taken on
report. Query: *vehicle flood stability instability criterion lateral displacement distance
threshold versus incipient velocity depth-velocity product*. Top three, all velocity or
depth-velocity formulations, none using displacement distance:

- **Gou et al. 2022**, *Urban Climate*: derives an explicit **incipient velocity** expression
  via Shannon and Tsallis entropy, validated against five experimental datasets.
- **Xia et al. 2013**, *Natural Hazards*: states directly that "the criterion of vehicle
  stability is usually represented by the **incipient velocity** for the vehicle."
- **Martínez-Gomariz et al. 2018**, *JFRM*, a state-of-the-art review of flooded-vehicle
  stability criteria, concluding the **AR&R** criterion is the safest available.

Combined with item 1, the 0.05 m figure is confirmed as an **internal numerical
onset-of-motion detector**, correctly citable to Xia and Shah for the underlying sliding
physics and to no one for the number. That is exactly how the paper now frames it.

## 3. CLOSED. NWS pedestrian versus vehicle depth

The NWS page, fetched live in the daytime audit at HTTP 200, says:

> "A mere 6 inches of fast-moving flood water can knock over an adult. It takes just 12 inches
> of rushing water to carry away most cars and just 2 feet of rushing water can carry away
> SUVs and trucks."

6 in = 0.1524 m is the **pedestrian** figure; 12 in = 0.3048 m is the **vehicle** figure. The
paper resolves this at `conference_101719_1.tex` line 123, Table I, verbatim from
`overleaf/main`:

> "Fixed depth cutoff at 0.15 m, independent of velocity or vehicle class. This is the
> National Weather Service's 6-inch figure, the depth at which fast-moving water can knock
> over an adult; the same source's vehicle figure is twice that, 12 inches (0.30 m), at which
> rushing water carries away most cars. We adopt the pedestrian depth deliberately, as the
> conservative bound of the two, and note that full-scale testing places small-car
> destabilization near the higher value. Across the 70-scenario grid this admits 7 scenarios;
> the 0.30 m cutoff would admit 21."

Both figures are stated, the choice is justified as deliberate and conservative, and its cost
is quantified as 7 versus 21 of 70. The original worry, that "either the threshold or the
framing needs to move," was answered by moving the framing.

Sub-questions 1 to 3 of the original item (is there a vehicle-specific 0.15 m figure anywhere;
what is the primary source behind "12 inches"; is the 6/12/24-inch trio a study or a
heuristic) are now **moot**, because the paper no longer presents 0.15 m as a vehicle
threshold. It presents it as the pedestrian bound, adopted knowingly.

It reports **floating instability at a flow depth of 0.38 m** and **sliding once D × V exceeds
0.36 m²/s**, over Froude 0.09 to 2.46. This is an **independent second D × V threshold from a
different method** than AR&R's flume work, and the 0.38 m flotation depth **corroborates the
Fig. 4 flotation argument** from an entirely separate direction.

A Scite full-text excerpt gives its analytic flotation formula directly:

> "the floating depth calculated from (Equation (13)) was 0.368 m, while the floating depth
> that was obtained numerically in this study was 0.380 m ... h_b = M_c / (ρ l_c b_c) + GC
> where hb is the buoyancy depth, Mc is the vehicle weight, ρ is the fluid density, lc and bc
> are the length and width of the vehicle, respectively, GC is the vehicle ground clearance"

Note the dependency: **Equation (13) requires ground clearance**, the same quantity item 8
leaves open. Closing A5 would let this paper's Fig. 4 flotation depth be checked against an
independent published analytic formula instead of resting on its own prism assumption.

A distinct, adjacent paper also reports 0.38 m: **Al-Qadami et al. 2022**,
`10.1111/jfr3.12828`, *JFRM* 15(4), giving critical depth 0.38 m but **D × V = 0.39 m²/s**, for
a *moving* vehicle. Do not conflate the two. The 0.36 m²/s figure belongs to the 2023
Sustainability paper.
